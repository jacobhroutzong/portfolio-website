Leak Protection for Older Homes

Older homes carry more water risk: aging supply lines, galvanized pipes, pipes in exterior walls and finished basements that weren't designed to stay dry. An automatic shutoff valve changes the outcome from a flooded floor to a phone alert.

Illustrative scenario

A 1960s ranch with a finished basement has a 15-year-old water heater and a washer on the main floor. Our partner plumber installs a flow-monitoring valve on the main line; we put leak sensors at the water heater, washer, two sinks, the dishwasher and the sump, and freeze sensors on the two exterior-wall pipe runs. A test shutoff confirms the valve closes in under a minute.

Checklist

  • Locate the main line and check valve access before quoting
  • Put sensors at the water heater, washer, every sink, dishwasher, fridge and sump
  • Add freeze sensors to pipes on exterior walls and in crawlspaces
  • Set "away" sensitivity for vacations and second homes
  • Ask your insurer about leak-device discounts and keep the install record

Worth knowing

  • Some insurers and states offer discounts for automatic shutoffs; programs differ, so confirm with your agent.
  • Valve installation on the main line is licensed plumbing work in most areas.
  • A shutoff protects against bursts, but sensors still matter for slow drips under fixtures.

Questions homeowners ask

How fast does an automatic valve close?

It depends on the model and settings. It closes when flow matches a burst or a leak pattern, and immediately when a linked sensor detects water.
