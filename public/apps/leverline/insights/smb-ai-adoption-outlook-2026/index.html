SMB AI Adoption in 2026 and Where Demand Goes Next

What the latest Census, Federal Reserve, Gartner and industry surveys say about small-business AI adoption, and our outlook for which AI use cases businesses will fund through 2028.

By Leverline ResearchPublished October 21, 20267 min read

Short version: depending on how you ask the question, somewhere between one in five and three in four US small businesses "use AI." The gap between those numbers is the real story, and the real opportunity. Most businesses have tried AI tools; very few have built AI into the workflows where money is made and lost.

Why adoption numbers disagree

The strictest measure comes from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey, which asks whether a business used AI in any business function in the past two weeks. As of May 2026 the national rate was about 19.8%, with the Information (39.7%) and Finance and Insurance (33.9%) sectors well above average. Between December 2025 and May 2026, use rose among firms with 20 or more employees but was flat among smaller firms (Census Bureau).

Broader surveys that count any use of AI tools land much higher. Goldman Sachs' 10,000 Small Businesses Voices survey, fielded in early 2026, found 76% of small businesses use AI in some form, but only 14% have it fully embedded in core operations (summary via Delaware Inc.). Thryv's 2026 survey put adoption at 66%, up from 55% a year earlier, with 70% of owners saying they need more training to use it well (WICPA). Intuit's 2026 AI Impact Report found more than three in four US small and midsize businesses now use AI regularly, led by marketing, admin and customer service (Intuit).

The Federal Reserve noted the same spread: Census data put firm-level adoption around 18% at the end of 2025, while an employment-weighted measure from the Atlanta Fed's Survey of Business Uncertainty was around 78%, because large employers adopt first (Federal Reserve).

The gap: trying AI vs. running on AI

Put those numbers together and a clear pattern emerges:

  • Individual use is common. Owners and staff use chat assistants to draft emails, posts and summaries.
  • Operational use is rare. Few businesses have AI answering their phones, processing their documents, scoring their leads or forecasting their inventory.
  • Skills, not interest, are the bottleneck. Training needs and lack of expertise show up across surveys as the leading barriers.

The bottleneck is implementation: choosing the right workflow, connecting it to existing systems, measuring the result and keeping it working.

Where the money is going

Gartner's September 2026 forecast puts worldwide AI spending at roughly $2.7 trillion for 2026, up about 50% year over year, with infrastructure the largest share (Gartner). Within that, AI services spending is forecast at about $576 billion in 2026 (Techstrong). Earlier in the year Gartner also described AI as sitting in the "trough of disillusionment" through 2026, arguing that ROI must become more predictable before enterprises scale up (Gartner, January 2026).

Larger organizations show the same "pilot to production" problem. McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey found 88% of organizations use AI in at least one function, but no more than 10% report scaling AI agents in any single function (McKinsey).

Our outlook: what businesses will fund through 2028

This is our view, based on the data above and what we see in the market. Treat it as a planning input, not a prediction.

Use case Demand outlook Why
AI voice agents & receptionists Very strong Revenue-recovery math is simple, deployment takes weeks and costs keep falling.
Document & workflow automation Strong, steady Labor constraints plus agents that can finally handle messy inputs.
Private & local LLMs Rising fast in regulated sectors Confidentiality, HIPAA and CMMC obligations, and capable open-weight models.
Natural-language analytics Rising in the mid-market Snowflake and Databricks ship native tools, but accuracy depends on semantic layers someone has to build.
Lead engines & ad signal work Strong Privacy changes make first-party data and conversion APIs essential.
Governance & training Steady Most owners want training, and regulation is arriving, if slower than expected.
Custom AI products Growing Software companies need AI features that hold up in production and protect margins.

On regulation: the EU's Digital Omnibus moved most high-risk AI Act obligations to December 2027 (Gibson Dunn), and Colorado's AI Act was revised with its effective date pushed to January 2027 (Checkr). Delays buy time; they don't remove the need for basic governance.

What this means if you're deciding where to start

  1. Start where the ROI math is simplest. Missed calls, re-keyed documents and slow lead follow-up have obvious baselines.
  2. Measure before you build. Agree on one success metric and record the baseline first.
  3. Budget for adoption, not just software. Training and change management decide whether a tool becomes a habit.
  4. Keep sensitive data private. If confidentiality is a concern, a private model removes the question entirely.
